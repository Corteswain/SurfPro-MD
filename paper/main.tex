\abstract{%
Surfactant property prediction currently requires either costly wet-lab measurements or computationally expensive molecular dynamics simulations, creating a bottleneck for high-throughput screening and data-driven molecular design.
We present SurfPro-MD, an open-source surrogate modelling tool that predicts nine physicochemical surfactant properties directly from SMILES representations in milliseconds, requiring no experimental data or simulation expertise.
The tool covers both experimentally reported properties --- critical micelle concentration (pCMC), surface tension at the CMC ($\gamma_{\mathrm{CMC}}$), surface excess concentration ($\Gamma_{\max}$), minimum molecular area ($A_{\min}$), and adsorption efficiency (pC$_{20}$) --- and simulation-derived properties --- solute and solvent diffusion coefficients ($D_{\mathrm{MOL}}$, $D_{\mathrm{SOL}}$), bulk viscosity ($\eta$), and interfacial surface tension ($\gamma$).
The underlying dataset combines curated experimental data from the SurfPro database\cite{hodl_surfpro_2025} with four additional properties generated from systematic atomistic MD simulations across 1436 surfactant molecules.
Surrogate models are implemented as ensembles of XGBoost regressors trained on RDKit molecular descriptors and evaluated using a cluster-based data splitting strategy that provides realistic estimates of generalisation to chemically novel compounds.
The tool is designed for direct integration into virtual screening pipelines and as a fast scoring function in generative molecular design frameworks.
Uncertainty estimation is provided through ensemble disagreement, nearest-neighbour applicability domain analysis, and conformal prediction intervals;
per-prediction SHAP feature attribution is included in the supplementary material, allowing users to assess not only what a molecule's predicted properties are, but how reliably and for what structural reasons.
All data, models, and code are openly available.
}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

Surfactants, a portmanteau of ``surface-active agents'', are chemical compounds that modulate interfacial properties between phases, including liquid–gas, liquid–liquid, and liquid–solid interfaces\cite{jia_review_2024}.
Their ability to control surface tension, wetting behaviour, and aggregation makes them essential components in detergents and cleaning formulations, food and cosmetic emulsions, enhanced oil recovery, pharmaceutical drug delivery, and semiconductor process chemistry\cite{sharma_safer_2023,chen_emerging_2024}.
In each of these settings, selecting the right surfactant for a given application requires evaluating a combination of physicochemical properties, often across a large number of candidates.

The practical bottleneck is measurement speed.
Experimental characterisation of surfactant properties is labour-intensive and highly system-specific\cite{katritzky_qspr_2007,hu_review_2010}.
Molecular dynamics (MD) simulations can access properties that are difficult to measure experimentally, but they are computationally expensive and require specialist expertise, making them impractical for routine candidate evaluation or large-scale library screening\cite{qazi_dynamic_2020,chen_molecular_2025}.
Neither approach integrates naturally into the tight evaluation loops of modern computational design pipelines, where a scoring function may be queried thousands of times per optimisation run.

Machine learning surrogate models offer a practical alternative: once trained on a sufficient dataset, they return property predictions in milliseconds from nothing more than a molecular structure, requiring no measurement or simulation.
Building reliable surfactant surrogates is, however, complicated by the fragmented state of available data.
Experimental datasets are limited in coverage and typically report only subsets of relevant properties, while MD-derived data are rarely integrated into unified modelling frameworks\cite{thacker_can_2023,brozos_graph_2024,nnadili_surfactant-specific_2024,hodl_surfpro_2025}.
No freely available tool currently exists that delivers multi-property surfactant predictions from molecular structure in a single call.

This work closes that gap.
We introduce SurfPro-MD, an open-source surrogate modelling tool that predicts nine surfactant physicochemical properties directly from SMILES representations, requiring no experimental data or simulation expertise.
Starting from the SurfPro database\cite{hodl_surfpro_2025}, we construct an augmented dataset of 1436 molecules by adding four properties generated from systematic atomistic MD simulations: solute and solvent diffusion coefficients, bulk viscosity, and interfacial surface tension.
Surrogate models trained on this combined dataset are evaluated using a cluster-based protocol that enforces chemical dissimilarity between training and test compounds, ensuring that reported performance reflects generalisation to genuinely novel structures.

The tool is designed for two primary use cases.
The first is virtual screening: researchers in academia or industry can evaluate a library of candidate surfactants across all nine properties in seconds, without committing resources to synthesis or simulation.
The second is generative molecular design, where SurfPro-MD can serve as a fast scoring function to evaluate and rank molecule proposals during optimisation.
Both use cases benefit from the tool's built-in uncertainty diagnostics---nearest-neighbour applicability domain analysis, ensemble disagreement, and conformal prediction intervals---and from the SHAP-based attribution analysis reported in the supplementary material, which together allow users to assess not only what the predicted properties are, but how reliably the model is operating and which structural features are responsible.

This work makes three principal contributions.
First, we extend the SurfPro database\cite{hodl_surfpro_2025} with four properties derived from systematic atomistic MD simulations across 1436 surfactant molecules, producing SurfPro-MD: a unified dataset combining experimentally measured and simulation-derived physicochemical properties for the same set of compounds, all freely available alongside the trained models and code.
Second, we develop and release an ensemble of XGBoost surrogate models that predict all nine properties from SMILES in milliseconds, with no simulation or experimental data required for a query, enabling direct integration into virtual screening pipelines and generative design workflows.
Third, we provide a comprehensive evaluation of model reliability, combining a cluster-based splitting protocol---which enforces chemical dissimilarity between training and test compounds to give realistic out-of-distribution performance estimates---with three complementary uncertainty diagnostics and a full SHAP-based feature attribution analysis, together characterising where, why, and to what extent predictions can be trusted.

All code, simulation workflows, and trained models are available via a public GitHub repository \url{https://github.com/Corteswain/SurfPro-MD} to ensure full reproducibility and facilitate further development.

\subsection{Dataset}

\paragraph{Curation.}
We base our work on the SurfPro\cite{hodl_surfpro_2025} database, a recently released curated collection of 1623 surfactant molecules covering eight structural categories: anionic, cationic, non-ionic, zwitterionic, and their gemini variants, including a sub-category of sugar-based non-ionic surfactants.
For each molecule, SurfPro provides up to six properties derived from experimental surface tension--concentration measurements: the critical micelle concentration (pCMC), surface tension at the CMC ($\gamma_{\mathrm{CMC}}$), surface pressure at the CMC ($\Pi_{\mathrm{CMC}}$), maximum surface excess concentration ($\Gamma_{\max}$), minimum molecular area at the air--water interface ($A_{\min}$), and adsorption efficiency (pC$_{20}$).
Of these, pCMC, $\gamma_{\mathrm{CMC}}$, and $\Pi_{\mathrm{CMC}}$ are read directly from the experimental $\gamma$--concentration curve, while $\Gamma_{\max}$, $A_{\min}$, and pC$_{20}$ are quantities derived from the same curve via the Gibbs adsorption isotherm;
all six are grounded in experimental measurements.
Property coverage is non-uniform: only 647 of the 1623 entries have all six properties measured, and several properties are reported for fewer than half of the entries.
The overall curation pipeline is shown in Figure~\ref{fig:overview}.

To this experimentally-derived foundation we add four properties obtained from atomistic MD simulations performed in this work (full simulation protocol described in the next section): the solute self-diffusion coefficient ($D_{\mathrm{MOL}}$), the solvent self-diffusion coefficient ($D_{\mathrm{SOL}}$), the bulk shear viscosity ($\eta$), and the air--water surface tension ($\gamma$).
In contrast to the experimental properties, these MD-derived values are computed for \textit{every} curated entry, providing complete coverage across the dataset.
The combined set of nine properties (five experimental, four MD-derived;
note that surface tension appears in both forms, $\gamma_{\mathrm{CMC}}$ from experiment and $\gamma$ from simulation) constitutes the SurfPro-MD dataset used throughout this work.

Several quality-control filters were applied to the original SurfPro entries before MD simulation.
A small number of entries (85 of 1623) contained invalid or chemically unreasonable SMILES strings and were removed, leaving 1538 entries.
Salts were stripped of their counterions, and all SMILES strings were canonicalised using RDKit (version 2025.09.3) \cite{landrum2006rdkit}.
Some surfactant cores were present multiple times in the original dataset under different counterions;
to ensure consistency with our MD simulations, which use sodium and chloride as the only counterions, we retained a single representative entry per unique core, preferring the form most consistent with our counterion choice.
Removing duplicates arising from alternative counterions yielded the final set of 1436 unique surfactant molecules used in all subsequent analyses.
To inform property selection and identify potential redundancy, target-property distributions and pairwise Pearson correlations were computed across the curated dataset and are shown in Figure~\ref{fig:data}\textbf{a} and \textbf{b}, respectively.

\paragraph{Splitting.}

For each of the nine target properties to be learned by the XGBoost models, a target-specific subset of the data was constructed.  
Since not all entries contain values for all properties, each subset was filtered to include only those data points with available values for the respective target.  
All entries contain values for the MD-derived properties (diffusion coefficients of solvent and solute, viscosity, and surface tension).

For properties exhibiting extreme values, outliers were removed on a per-subset basis.
Cutoffs were set conservatively by examining the empirical distributions (Figure~\ref{fig:data}\textbf{a}) and identifying values that fell as isolated points far outside the bulk of the distribution, inconsistent with the range expected for physically plausible measurements.
The aim was to exclude likely artefacts while retaining as much data as possible given the limited dataset size.

To obtain robust estimates of model performance, a cluster-based splitting strategy was employed.  
Molecules were grouped into chemically similar clusters using the Butina clustering algorithm (threshold $= 0.6$) based on Tanimoto similarity. 
This threshold was chosen to balance two competing requirements of cluster-based splitting. 
On the one hand, sufficiently large clusters are required to construct statistically meaningful train, validation, and test sets. 
On the other hand, clusters should be chemically distinct to ensure a realistic assessment of model generalisation. 
The chosen threshold yields a manageable number of 91 clusters while maintaining meaningful separation between them, thereby avoiding both overly homogeneous splits and excessive fragmentation of the dataset.

For each target property, 5 independent holdout test sets were constructed by assigning whole clusters to the test set until a predefined size range (7--11\% of all molecules) was reached.  
Clusters were selected randomly, and only accepted if their inclusion resulted in a test set within this size range.

For each test set, the remaining clusters were used to generate 5 independent train/validation splits using the same procedure, again enforcing a validation set size between 12\% and 18\% of all molecules.  
This resulted in 25 distinct train/validation/test combinations per target property.

This cluster-based splitting ensures that structurally similar molecules are not simultaneously present in training and test sets, thereby providing a more realistic assessment of model generalisation to novel chemical structures.


\subsection{MD Simulations}
\label{sec:methods:molecular_dynamics}

As no 3D structures or force field parameterisations were available for the original SurfPro database, the first step in processing the data was the conversion from SMILES strings to three-dimensional molecular structures.
Structure generation was performed using Open Babel 3.0.1 \cite{oboyle_open_2011} with the {\tt --gen3d} flag and default parameters, producing initial structures in Protein Data Bank (PDB) format.
Hydrogen atoms were added automatically, and stereochemistry was inferred directly from the SMILES representation.
No additional geometry optimisation was performed beyond the default Open Babel force-field relaxation.

To enable MD simulations, all molecules were parameterised using the GAFF2 force field \cite{wang_development_2004} and AM1-BCC charges \cite{jakalian_fast_2002} via ANTECHAMBER from the AmberTools package \cite{case_ambertools_2023}.
Protonation states were taken directly from the input SMILES strings, without additional pKa-based corrections.
Topology files compatible with GROMACS were generated using ACPYPE \cite{sousa_da_silva_acpype_2012}.
Further system preparation and all simulations were carried out using GROMACS 2024.3 \cite{lindahl_gromacs_2001,abraham_gromacs_2015}.

For each system, 150 solute molecules were initially inserted into a cubic simulation box of 16\,nm per side.
The system was subsequently equilibrated using extensive NPT simulations, during which the box size was reduced to its final density.
This equilibration ensured physically meaningful densities prior to production simulations.
After equilibration, the final systems contained 15,000 TIP4P/2005 water molecules \cite{abascal_general_2005}.
Charge neutrality was enforced by adding sodium or chloride counterions as required.

The surfactant concentration employed here --- 150 solute molecules co-solvated with 15,000 water molecules --- substantially exceeds the CMC of most surfactants and was a deliberate design choice.
At concentrations near or below the CMC, bulk properties such as diffusion coefficients and viscosity differ only subtly between structurally distinct compounds, and resolving those differences reliably would require prohibitively long simulations.
By operating well above the CMC, property differences between molecules are large and clearly separable, yielding a dataset with strong and unambiguous training signal for comparative ranking across chemical space.
These simulations are therefore not intended to reproduce real-life application conditions;
they generate internally consistent, relative property values that discriminate between surfactant structures.

To verify that this concentration regime does not introduce artefacts from micelle formation, we visually inspected trajectory snapshots for a representative subset of systems.
No stable micellar structures were observed on our 10\,ns simulation timescale.
Full micelle equilibration is known to require timescales several orders of magnitude longer --- typically hundreds of microseconds --- and all systems therefore remain in a dispersed or early pre-aggregation state throughout the production window.
Diffusion coefficients and viscosity computed from these trajectories therefore reflect single-molecule and short-range collective dynamics rather than the behaviour of assembled micelles.

The system was energy-minimised using the steepest descent algorithm until a maximum force below 1000\,kJ\,mol$^{-1}$\,nm$^{-1}$ was reached.
Temperature was controlled using a velocity-rescaling thermostat ($\tau = 0.1$\,ps), and pressure was maintained using the C-rescale barostat ($\tau = 2.0$\,ps, compressibility $4.5 \times 10^{-5}$\,bar$^{-1}$).
All production simulations were performed in the NPT ensemble unless stated otherwise.
All bonds involving hydrogen atoms were constrained using the LINCS algorithm \cite{hess_lincs_1997}.
Short-range van der Waals interactions were truncated at 1.0\,nm, and long-range electrostatics were treated using the Particle Mesh Ewald (PME) method with a real-space cutoff of 1.0\,nm.

Following equilibration, the system was propagated for 10\,ns with configurations saved every 500\,ps, yielding multiple restart points used for replica generation.

From these configurations, two types of production simulations were initiated.
The first consisted of non-equilibrium shear simulations for viscosity determination.
Shear flow was induced using a cosine-shaped acceleration profile applied along the $x$-direction as a function of the $z$-position of each atom.
The maximum acceleration was 0.02\,nm\,ps$^{-1}$.
Viscosity was computed using the implementation provided in GROMACS 2024.3, following the built-in shear viscosity protocol.
Each simulation was run for 10\,ns and repeated for 10 replicas per system.

The second simulation type was used to compute surface tension.
Starting from the same restart configurations, the simulation box was elongated in the $z$-direction to 30\,nm, creating a symmetric slab geometry consisting of a water/surfactant phase separated by two vacuum interfaces.
The slab thickness was identical to the pre-expansion box length, resulting in a system-specific vacuum gap that was significantly larger than the liquid region.
These simulations were equilibrated for 1\,ns followed by 10\,ns of production dynamics.
Surface tension was computed using the standard pressure-tensor route.
No slab-specific long-range electrostatic correction (e.g., Yeh--Berkowitz correction) was applied.

For these slab simulations, the barostat was disabled to prevent artificial compression of the interface, which would otherwise relax the system back toward a cubic box geometry.

These simulations yielded diffusion coefficients for solute and solvent, as well as viscosity and surface tension values for each system.
Together, this data forms the extended SurfPro-MD dataset.

To assess the physical validity of the simulation protocol, viscosity and surface tension were additionally computed for small-molecule systems with available experimental reference data.
Viscosity calculations were performed for aqueous solutions of formic, acetic, propionic, and valeric acid.
Surface tension calculations were performed for methanol, ethanol, and propanol.
For each system, multiple concentrations were simulated (150, 300, 450, 1500, and 2500 solute molecules in 15,000 water molecules) to probe concentration-dependent behaviour.
Experimental comparisons were made using both absolute and relative deviations of the simulated properties.
The number of available experimental data points varied depending on literature availability, as these data were not generated in this work.


\subsection{Surrogate Models for Surfactant Properties}
\label{sec:methods:surrogate}

To deliver fast, accessible surfactant property predictions, we implement SurfPro-MD as a surrogate modelling tool built on gradient-boosted regression models.
Given a SMILES string as input, the tool returns predicted values for all nine target properties together with ensemble-based uncertainty estimates derived from the multi-split training procedure;
no prior simulation or experimental data is required for a query.

Gradient-boosted decision tree models were implemented using the XGBoost framework \cite{chen_xgboost_2016,boldini_practical_2023}, which is well suited for learning non-linear structure--property relationships in medium-sized chemical datasets.
All models were trained using the standard squared-error regression objective.
Molecular structures were encoded using the full set of 217 physicochemical descriptors computed with RDKit's molecular descriptors module (version 2025.09.3) \cite{landrum2006rdkit}, capturing a broad range of constitutional, topological, and physicochemical properties.

For each target property, model evaluation was performed using the train/validation/test partitions described in the Methods.
Briefly, for each property, 5 independent holdout test sets were constructed.
For each test set, 5 corresponding train/validation splits were generated from the remaining data.

Hyperparameter optimisation was carried out independently for each train/validation split using Bayesian optimisation with Optuna.\cite{optuna_2019} The optimised parameters included the learning rate, maximum tree depth, and number of boosting iterations (learning rate: 0.01--0.3, maximum depth: 3--10, number of estimators: 100--500), while all other XGBoost parameters were kept at their default values.

For a given test set, the resulting 5 optimised models (one per train/validation split) were combined into an ensemble, and predictions on the holdout test set were obtained by averaging the individual model outputs.
This procedure was repeated for all 5 test sets per target property, ensuring that reported performance metrics are not dependent on a single arbitrary split but instead reflect average performance across multiple statistically independent dataset partitions.

Further implementation details, including the full optimisation setup and model configuration, are provided in the accompanying GitHub repository to ensure full reproducibility.
To characterise which molecular descriptors drive predictions, permutation importance and SHAP-based feature attribution analyses were carried out for all nine targets;
results are reported in the Supporting Information.
To verify that RDKit descriptors represent the optimal molecular representation for this task, we additionally compared them against Morgan circular fingerprints and two PCA-compressed variants;
this ablation study is described fully in the Supporting Information.


\subsection{Uncertainty Estimation}
\label{sec:methods:uncertainty}

Three complementary uncertainty diagnostics are evaluated for SurfPro-MD, each targeting a different aspect of prediction reliability.

\paragraph{Applicability domain via nearest-neighbour distance.}
To flag predictions that require extrapolation beyond the training distribution, we compute, for each test molecule, the Euclidean distance to its nearest neighbour in the training set within the standardised RDKit feature space.
A large nearest-neighbour (NN) distance indicates structural novelty relative to the training data and is expected to correlate with increased prediction error.
We evaluate this relationship using the Spearman rank correlation $\rho$ between NN distances and absolute test errors across all five test splits per property.
Points with NN distance exceeding 40 (in standardised feature space) are excluded as structural isolates.

\paragraph{Ensemble disagreement.}
Each property model comprises an ensemble of five XGBoost regressors trained on distinct train/validation partitions of the same test split.
The standard deviation of the five individual model predictions serves as a per-molecule uncertainty estimate reflecting the sensitivity of the prediction to the choice of training data.
A high ensemble standard deviation indicates substantial disagreement among models, which is expected to correlate with larger prediction errors.
We assess this relationship via Spearman correlation and by comparing ensemble standard deviation directly against absolute test error.

\paragraph{Conformal prediction.}
To translate uncertainty estimates into prediction intervals with statistical coverage guarantees, we implement cross-conformal regression \cite{vovk_algorithmic_2005,angelopoulos_gentle_2023}.
For each target property and each of the five test splits, the absolute residuals $|\hat{y}_i - y_i|$ from each fold model evaluated on its held-out validation set are pooled across all five folds to form a calibration set of nonconformity scores.
For a nominal coverage level $1-\alpha$, the conformal quantile is
\begin{equation}
    q = \mathrm{Quantile}\!\left(\{s_i\},\;\min\!\left(1,\,(1-\alpha)\!\left(1+\frac{1}{n_\mathrm{cal}}\right)\right)\right),
    \label{eq:conformal_quantile}
\end{equation}
where $n_\mathrm{cal}$ is the calibration set size and the factor $(1+1/n_\mathrm{cal})$ provides the standard finite-sample coverage correction \cite{angelopoulos_gentle_2023}.
The resulting prediction interval for a new molecule with predicted value $\hat{y}$ is $[\hat{y}-q,\,\hat{y}+q]$.

Calibration is assessed by plotting nominal coverage $(1-\alpha)$ against empirical coverage --- the fraction of test molecules whose true value falls within the corresponding interval --- swept across 60 values of $\alpha$.
A perfectly calibrated model lies on the diagonal;
deviations quantify the degree to which the exchangeability assumption underlying conformal prediction is violated by the cluster-based splitting strategy.


\section{Results}
\label{results}

\subsection{Dataset Analysis}
\label{sec:results:dataset_analysis}

The distribution of values for each target property in our SurfPro-MD augmented dataset is shown in Figure~\ref{fig:data}\textbf{a}.
Several properties exhibit values that lie far outside the main distribution.
Especially in the low-data regime, artefacts can severely affect model performance.
As it cannot be determined unambiguously whether these represent true physical outliers or experimental artefacts, these data points were treated conservatively in the splitting and modelling stages and were removed on a per-subset basis.
Cutoffs were set conservatively by identifying values that fell as isolated points far outside the bulk of the distribution (Figure~\ref{fig:data}\textbf{a}), inconsistent with the range expected for physically plausible measurements, with the aim of excluding likely artefacts while retaining as much data as possible.

Since many surfactant-relevant properties are physically interrelated, we examined their statistical dependence via the pairwise Pearson correlation matrix shown in Figure~\ref{fig:data}\textbf{b}.
A near-perfect anticorrelation ($-0.99$) is observed between $\gamma_{\mathrm{CMC}}$ and $\Pi_{\mathrm{CMC}}$, reflecting their direct linear relationship.
All entries with $\Pi_{\mathrm{CMC}}$ values are also a subset of those with $\gamma_{\mathrm{CMC}}$ values, so $\Pi_{\mathrm{CMC}}$ provides no additional information and was excluded from further analysis.
The adsorption efficiency pC$_{20}$ shows moderate correlation with pCMC and moderate anticorrelation with the solute diffusion coefficient $D_{\mathrm{MOL}}$;
these relationships, however, are not strong enough to justify excluding any of these properties.
The low correlation between the simulation-derived surface tension ($\gamma$) and the experimentally reported $\gamma_{\mathrm{CMC}}$ is expected and physically meaningful: $\gamma$ is computed at the high concentrations used in our simulations, where the air--water interface is already densely populated, while $\gamma_{\mathrm{CMC}}$ is measured specifically at the CMC, the concentration at which the interface first reaches saturation.
These two quantities report on different physicochemical regimes and are treated as independent targets throughout this work.

\subsection{Molecular Dynamics Validation}
\label{sec:results:molecular_dynamics}

To assess the reliability of the overall MD protocol—including force field assignment, system preparation, equilibration strategy, and production simulation settings—we performed additional simulations on chemically well-characterised reference systems.
These systems were not part of the SurfPro database and were selected to provide independent validation of the simulation workflow.

Short-chain alcohols (methanol, ethanol, propanol) and carboxylic acids (formic, acetic, propionic, valeric acid) were selected as reference compounds for three reasons.
First, they form well-characterised homologous series for which reliable experimental data is available across a range of compositions.
Second, the systematic increase in chain length produces monotone, clearly resolved differences in both surface tension and viscosity, providing a stringent test of the model's ability to recover structure--property trends.
Third, unlike actual surfactants, these simple molecules do not undergo micellisation or interface-specific self-assembly on the timescales accessible to our simulations, so a direct quantitative comparison with experiment at our simulation conditions is possible.
For actual surfactants, molecules with similar structures tend to have very similar properties, while molecules with clearly distinct experimental responses differ so substantially in structure that no clean homologous comparison is available---making it impossible to construct the kind of systematic, monotone validation series used here.

Figure~\ref{fig:MD}\textbf{a} shows the concentration-dependent surface tension for aqueous solutions of methanol, ethanol, and propanol.
The simulations correctly reproduce the relative changes in surface tension as a function of composition and maintain the ordering between homologous systems.
For methanol and ethanol, the experimental reference lies well within the simulations' error bars, whereas the propanol simulation slightly overestimates the reduction in surface tension at higher concentrations.
Small systematic deviations in magnitude are observed, which is expected given known limitations of the TIP4P/2005 water model in reproducing absolute interfacial properties.

A similar trend is observed for viscosity (Figure~\ref{fig:MD}\textbf{b}).
The simulations reproduce the experimentally observed trends for aqueous solutions of formic, acetic, propionic, and valeric acid, capturing both the increase in viscosity with concentration and the relative ordering between different solutes.
Since the primary objective of this work is comparative screening of surfactants, preserving relative trends across chemical space is more relevant than reproducing absolute experimental values, and the concentration-dependent agreement observed in both panels confirms that our simulation framework fulfils this requirement.

Overall, these results indicate that the employed MD protocol provides a consistent and physically reasonable description of concentration-dependent structural and dynamical behaviour across different chemical systems.
In particular, the ability to reproduce relative property changes across composition suggests that the simulation framework is suitable for generating internally consistent training data for surrogate modelling.

The overall distributions of MD-derived and experimental properties are shown in Figure~\ref{fig:data}\textbf{a}.
Several properties exhibit outliers that lie far outside the main distribution.
These values are retained in the dataset for data splitting but excluded from model training on a per-target basis when they fall outside physically reasonable ranges.
The majority of extreme viscosity values, in particular, are likely attributable to experimental uncertainty, as viscosity measurements become increasingly challenging for highly viscous systems.

\paragraph{Overall predictive performance.}
The performance of the surrogate models across all nine target properties is summarised in Table~\ref{tab:results:model_performance} and visualised in Figure~\ref{fig:results}\textbf{a}.
Results are reported as mean and standard deviation across all independent train/validation/test partitions (see Methods), providing a robust estimate of model performance with respect to dataset partitioning.
Model performance varies substantially across targets, with MD-derived properties generally easier to predict than the experimental ones;
we discuss each group in turn below.

\paragraph{MD-derived properties.}
Among the MD-derived properties, the diffusion coefficients of solute and solvent are predicted with high accuracy ($R^2 = 0.759 \pm 0.120$ and $0.920 \pm 0.012$, respectively), as is viscosity ($\eta$;
$R^2 = 0.818 \pm 0.044$).
Surface tension ($\gamma$) is more challenging, with a lower $R^2$ of $0.364 \pm 0.038$ but a moderate rank correlation ($\rho = 0.627 \pm 0.042$), indicating that, while absolute values are harder to capture, the model still preserves meaningful ordering across chemical space.
This pattern, where rank correlation remains informative even when $R^2$ is modest, recurs throughout our results and is particularly relevant for the screening use case targeted by this work, where the primary objective is to compare and prioritise candidate molecules rather than recover exact property values.


\paragraph{Experimentally-derived properties.}
pCMC model is the strongest in this group ($R^2 = 0.518 \pm 0.143$, $\rho = 0.731 \pm 0.099$).
In contrast, $\Gamma_{\max}$, $A_{\min}$, and pC$_{20}$ exhibit lower predictive performance, with $R^2$ values ranging from slightly negative to approximately $0.18$, although moderate rank correlations are still observed in most cases.
This $R^2$--$\rho$ disparity is at least partly attributable to the limited numerical resolution of these experimental properties, which contain a large number of repeated or discretised target values.
Since $R^2$ is defined relative to the mean-baseline predictor, datasets with concentrated value distributions yield strong baseline performance and thus systematically low $R^2$ even when a model captures meaningful predictive signal.
The higher Spearman correlations observed for these targets indicate that the models retain substantial ability to recover correct rank ordering despite the metric-level penalty.

\paragraph{Feature attribution and representation ablation.}
Permutation importance and SHAP-based feature attribution analyses were carried out for all nine targets.
Raw RDKit descriptors were found to outperform Morgan circular fingerprints and PCA-compressed variants across all targets.
Full results for both analyses are provided in the Supporting Information.

\paragraph{Comparison with prior SurfPro models and screening utility.}
Parity plots for all nine target properties are shown in Figure~\ref{fig:results}\textbf{c}--\textbf{j}. 
For visual clarity, each panel corresponds to a single ensemble (one test set and its associated models) per property;

because each ensemble is constructed from a different holdout test set, the displayed performance is not necessarily representative of the average performance reported in Table~\ref{tab:results:model_performance}. 
In particular, $A_{\min}$ and pC$_{20}$ perform better on average than their parity plots would suggest, reflecting variability between test sets rather than a systematic failure of the models. 
When evaluated under the same stratified splitting strategy used by Hödl et al.\cite{hodl_surfpro_2025}, our models achieve comparable predictive performance while additionally providing interpretable RDKit-based features, ensemble uncertainty estimates, and conformal prediction intervals. A detailed numerical comparison across split strategies is provided in the Supporting Information. Taken together, the moderate-to-high rank correlations across both MD-derived and experimental targets, combined with the consistency of these trends across independent data partitions, indicate that the SurfPro-MD surrogate models are well suited for comparative surfactant screening in both academic and industrial settings.

\paragraph{Nearest-neighbour distance and ensemble disagreement.}
Spearman correlations between NN distance and absolute error, and between ensemble standard deviation and absolute error, are summarised in Table~\ref{tab:conformal_coverage}.
The two heuristics are complementary: NN distance is most informative for pCMC and pC$_{20}$ ($\rho_{\mathrm{NN}} = 0.34$), while ensemble standard deviation provides the strongest signal for $D_{\mathrm{MOL}}$ ($\rho_{\mathrm{std}} = 0.45$), a target for which NN distance carries no predictive information ($\rho_{\mathrm{NN}} = -0.06$).
Per-molecule scatter plots for both heuristics are provided in the Supporting Information.

\paragraph{Conformal calibration.}
Figure~\ref{fig:unc_conformal} shows calibration curves for all nine target properties;
Table~\ref{tab:conformal_coverage} summarises empirical coverage at nominal levels from 50\% to 90\%.
The calibration behaviour follows a pattern that correlates with training-set size and model accuracy.

The three properties with the fewest training examples --- $\Gamma_{\max}$, $A_{\min}$, and pC$_{20}$, each available for fewer than 600 molecules --- are uniformly over-covered throughout the calibration range, with empirical coverage exceeding nominal by up to 14 percentage points at intermediate levels.
For these targets, the model's in-distribution difficulty already approximates its out-of-distribution difficulty, so calibration residuals from the validation folds are representative of test errors on novel clusters, yielding conservative intervals.

At the opposite extreme, the MD-derived targets $D_{\mathrm{SOL}}$ ($\Delta = -0.04$), $\eta$ ($\Delta = -0.05$), and $\gamma$ ($\Delta = -0.03$) are systematically under-covered, with the largest deviations again at intermediate nominal levels, where empirical coverage falls up to 13 percentage points below nominal.
For these high-accuracy targets, the model achieves tight in-distribution predictions, resulting in small calibration residuals;
when the resulting intervals are applied to test compounds from structurally dissimilar clusters, they are insufficiently wide to accommodate the inflated errors, and the nominal coverage guarantee is not met.

pCMC ($\Delta = -0.00$) and $D_{\mathrm{MOL}}$ ($\Delta = -0.00$) show near-perfect calibration throughout, and $\gamma_{\mathrm{CMC}}$ ($\Delta = -0.02$) shows mild under-coverage, despite an intermediate number of training examples (836).

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

The results presented above span three distinct levels of evaluation: the quality and coverage of the underlying dataset, the predictive accuracy of the surrogate models across nine physicochemical properties, and the reliability of the associated uncertainty estimates.
Taken together, they provide a coherent picture of what SurfPro-MD can and cannot be expected to deliver in practice.

Transport properties --- diffusion coefficients and viscosity --- are predicted with high accuracy ($R^2 > 0.75$, Spearman $\rho > 0.86$), and meaningful rank ordering is preserved across most remaining targets, with Spearman correlations of 0.5--0.7 for properties such as $\gamma_{\mathrm{CMC}}$ and pCMC.
Rank preservation matters as much as absolute accuracy here: a scoring function that correctly orders candidates will guide a generative model toward better molecules even when predicted values deviate from their experimental counterparts.

Like any surrogate model, SurfPro-MD operates within an applicability domain determined by the chemical diversity of its training set.
What distinguishes this tool is that this domain is made explicit rather than left to the user to infer.
Three complementary diagnostics are provided for every prediction: nearest-neighbour distance in the RDKit feature space flags structural extrapolation beyond the training distribution;
ensemble disagreement quantifies sensitivity to the choice of training data;
and conformal prediction intervals provide coverage-calibrated uncertainty bounds with known statistical properties.
For the best-predicted properties, these diagnostics give coherent and informative signals even for structurally novel compounds.
For harder targets --- notably $A_{\min}$, $\Gamma_{\max}$, and $\gamma$ --- where absolute accuracy is lower, the uncertainty estimates correctly reflect this difficulty, allowing users to distinguish confident from borderline predictions before committing to downstream experiments or simulations.

\paragraph{Uncertainty estimation and the role of distributional shift.}
The three uncertainty diagnostics reveal a coherent picture that connects model performance, applicability domain, and the structure of the chemical space covered by SurfPro-MD.

For the two uncertainty heuristics --- NN distance and ensemble disagreement --- the signal is strongest where model performance is highest.
This is not a coincidence: it reflects a well-known characteristic of gradient-boosted ensembles, which tend to agree confidently on interpolated predictions and disagree near the boundaries of the training distribution.
Properties with modest Spearman correlations ($\rho_{\mathrm{NN}}$ or $\rho_{\mathrm{std}} < 0.1$) --- most notably $\gamma$ and $\gamma_{\mathrm{CMC}}$ --- are targets where the model is wrong in ways that are not captured by either structural proximity or ensemble disagreement, indicating a more fundamental limitation of the feature representation or the model's inductive bias for these properties.

The conformal calibration curves (Figure~\ref{fig:unc_conformal}) show a pattern tied to training-set size and model accuracy.
Low-data experimental targets ($\Gamma_{\max}$, $A_{\min}$, pC$_{20}$) are consistently over-covered: because the model is similarly uncertain inside and outside the training distribution, calibration residuals are conservative for novel compounds.
High-accuracy MD targets ($D_{\mathrm{SOL}}$, $\eta$, $\gamma$) show the opposite tendency: under-coverage that is most pronounced at intermediate nominal levels, where tight in-distribution residuals underestimate the errors encountered on held-out structural clusters.
The practical implication is that conformal intervals for the data-poor experimental targets can be used at face value or treated as conservative, while users of the best-predicted MD targets requesting, for example, 90\% coverage should anticipate approximately 85\% actual coverage on structurally novel compounds.
A detailed per-target analysis is provided in the Supporting Information.

SurfPro-MD incorporates both of these diagnostics.
Applicability domain estimation is provided through the nearest-neighbour distance analysis described above, flagging predictions that require extrapolation beyond the training distribution.
SHAP-based feature attribution has been carried out for all nine targets and is reported in the supplementary material, decomposing each prediction into contributions from individual molecular descriptors and identifying which structural features are responsible for each property estimate.
Together, these components support informed use of the tool's predictions rather than requiring users to treat them as a black box.


\section{Conclusion}
We present SurfPro-MD, a practical open-source tool for multi-property surfactant prediction.
Given a SMILES string, the tool returns predictions for nine physicochemical properties in milliseconds, without requiring experimental measurements or molecular simulations.
It is designed for direct use in virtual screening workflows, where researchers in academia or industry need to compare candidate surfactants rapidly across multiple properties, and as a scoring function in generative molecular design, where fast and repeatable property evaluation is essential.

The tool is built on a dataset of 1436 curated surfactant molecules integrating experimental and MD-derived properties, and on an ensemble of XGBoost models developed with a cluster-based evaluation protocol that provides realistic estimates of generalisation to novel compounds.
Properties with strong predictive performance --- diffusion coefficients and viscosity --- are immediately suitable as quantitative screening criteria.
Properties with preserved but lower rank correlation remain useful for candidate prioritisation.

Applicability domain estimation and per-prediction SHAP attribution are already included, enabling users to assess prediction confidence and identify the molecular features responsible for each property estimate.
All data, models, and code are openly available to support reproducibility and further development.
